B What It Costs to Run Your Business

These are the costs you pay whether you are working or not. Replace the figures with your own. If you are not sure, leave the default — it is a realistic starting point.

Vehicle — fuel per year ? Add up what you spend on fuel for your work vehicle across the whole year. If you are not sure, think about how much you spend in a typical week and multiply by 48 (allowing for some time off).
Vehicle — insurance and registration ? Your work vehicle insurance and any registration or road tax costs per year. In most countries you need commercial vehicle insurance if you use your vehicle for work — personal car insurance usually does not cover this.
Vehicle — servicing and repairs ? What you spend on keeping your vehicle running — services, tyres, unexpected repairs. Even if you had a cheap year, a good year is followed by an expensive one. Use an average.
Vehicle — wear and tear reserve ? Your vehicle loses value every year you drive it. Eventually you will need to replace it. This is money you set aside each year toward that day. If you ignore this, the cost comes as a nasty surprise. Even 100 a month is better than nothing.
Public liability insurance ? This covers you if you accidentally damage a customer's property or someone gets injured while you are working. Most customers — especially any commercial or landlord work — will ask to see this before you start. It is not optional if you want to be taken seriously and be protected.
One cost not included above: income protection insurance. If you cannot work — a fall off a ladder, a bad back, a serious illness — your income stops the same day. There is no sick pay when you work for yourself. Income protection insurance pays you a monthly amount while you are unable to work. It is worth researching before you need it. ? Known as income protection insurance in the UK and Ireland, short-term disability insurance in the USA, and disability income insurance in Canada. For a painter it typically covers 50-65% of your average income after a waiting period of 4 to 13 weeks. The full paid tool includes this as a cost in your rate calculation and gives you country-specific guidance on where to get cover.
Tools — replacement and new purchases per year ? Brushes, rollers, poles, scrapers, dust sheets, spray equipment, power tools — all of it wears out or needs upgrading. Add up what you typically spend in a year. If this year was cheap, last year was probably expensive. Use an average.
Accountant or bookkeeper ? When you work for yourself you need to file a tax return every year. An accountant does this for you, keeps you out of trouble, and often saves you more than they cost. Even a basic annual fee is worth including here. If you do it yourself for free right now, that is fine — put zero, but know you may need this later.
Phone and data plan ? Your phone is a business tool. You use it for customer calls, messages, photos, navigation, and payments. Include the cost of your plan here.
Software and subscriptions ? Any apps, software, or online tools you pay for to run your business. This might include a quoting app, accounting software, cloud storage, or anything else. Add them up across the year.
Workwear and PPE ? Overalls, boots, dust masks, safety glasses — anything you wear or use to protect yourself on the job. These wear out and need replacing. Include what you spend in a year.
Training or licences ? Any courses, trade tests, certifications, or licences you need to keep current. In some countries you need a licence to trade legally — check what applies to you. Include renewal costs here.
Advertising and getting work ? Anything you spend to find customers or get your name out there. This could be Facebook ads, Google ads, leaflets, a website, business cards, or a listing on a local trades directory. If you are just starting and spending nothing yet, put zero — but know that finding work usually costs something, even if it is just your time.
Facebook ads, leaflets, website, trades directories — anything you spend to find customers.

D How Many Hours You Can Actually Charge For
Days you work per week ? How many days a week you actually paint? Most sole traders work 4 to 5 days. If you are just starting, be honest — some weeks will be slow at first while you build up work.
Most self-employed painters work 4 to 5 days. Be honest about your actual situation.
Weeks off per year (holidays, sick days, quiet weeks) ? This includes your holiday, any sick days, bank holidays, and slow weeks when you do not have work lined up. Most self-employed painters lose 6 to 10 weeks a year to this even if they are busy. If you guess low here, your rate will come out too low and you will slowly run out of money.
Include holidays, bank holidays, quiet weeks, and sick days. Most painters lose 6–10 weeks.
Hours per day spent actually painting ? This is not how long you are out of the house. It is the hours you are physically on the tools — brush in hand, roller on the wall. You cannot charge for driving to a job, giving a quote, buying materials, or cleaning up. Most painters get 5.5 to 7 chargeable hours out of an 8-hour day.
Hours with a brush in your hand. Not travel, not quoting, not cleaning up. Typically 5.5–7 hours.
Chargeable hours per year ? This is the total number of hours per year you can actually bill a customer for. It is calculated from your days per week, hours per day, and weeks working. This is the number your hourly rate is divided into — get this wrong and your rate will be wrong. —
